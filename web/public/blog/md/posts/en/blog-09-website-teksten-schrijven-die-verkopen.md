# Writing website copy that wins customers — 8 principles

A beautiful website without good copy is like a shop window without products. Visitors come in, look around, and leave — without taking action.

Yet most business owners spend 80% of their budget on design and technology, and 20% on copy. While it's the copy that determines whether someone calls you, requests a quote, or clicks through to a competitor.

These are eight principles that make the difference.

## 1. Write for your customer, not for yourself

The most common mistake on business websites: it's about the company, not the customer.

**Almost everyone writes this:**
> "We are an innovative company with more than 15 years of experience in developing high-quality solutions."

**This works better:**
> "Your website loads too slowly, you're losing customers to Google, and your current web developer no longer responds. Sound familiar? Then you're in the right place."

The difference: the first text tells what you do. The second tells what the customer experiences. Visitors aren't interested in your company history — they want to know if you can solve *their* problem.

**Rule of thumb:** count the word "we" on your homepage. If it appears more often than "you", rewrite it.

## 2. Start with the problem, not the solution

People aren't searching for "a new website". They're searching for a solution to a problem:

- "My website is slow and I'm losing customers"
- "I'm not found on Google"
- "My webshop looks unprofessional"
- "I'm paying too much for a site that doesn't work"

Start every page by naming that problem. If a visitor recognizes themselves, they'll read on. If you start with your solution, there's no hook to keep them.

## 3. Be specific

Vague copy says nothing. Specific copy convinces.

| Vague | Specific |
|---|---|
| "We build fast websites" | "Our sites load in 1.4 seconds — 3× faster than average" |
| "Affordable prices" | "Websites from €2,500, fixed price, no surprises" |
| "Years of experience" | "47 websites delivered since 2019" |
| "Satisfied customers" | "4.8 stars on Google (38 reviews)" |

Numbers, data, and concrete examples are more credible than superlatives. "The best websites" says nothing. "Under 1.5 second load time" says everything.

## 4. One page, one goal

Every page on your website should have one primary goal. The homepage: spark curiosity and direct visitors onward. The services page: explain what you do and trigger a quote request. The contact page: make filling in the form as easy as possible.

If a page tries to achieve three different things, it achieves none.

**In practice:**
- Determine per page what the visitor should do (call, fill in form, view product)
- Make that action clearly visible (call-to-action)
- Remove everything that distracts from that goal

## 5. Write for scanning

Nobody reads your website from start to finish. Visitors scan. They read headings, bold words, and the first sentence of each paragraph.

**Make your copy scannable:**

- **Short paragraphs** — maximum 3–4 sentences
- **Clear subheadings** — each section with a heading that summarizes the content
- **Bold key words** — so scanners pick up the essence
- **Bullet lists** — for lists, features, benefits
- **White space** — give the text room to breathe

A page that looks "light" at first glance gets read more often than a wall of text.

## 6. Use proof

Every claim you make without proof can be ignored by the visitor. Every claim with proof becomes credible.

**Forms of proof:**

- **Customer reviews:** "Exactly what we were looking for. Fast communication and a great result." — Jan, Timber Yard Meppel
- **Case studies:** "Jan Sok Timber Yard went from 0 to 2,500 visitors per month after launching the new website"
- **Numbers:** "Average load time: 1.3 seconds. Google PageSpeed score: 94/100"
- **Logos:** Show well-known clients or partners
- **Certifications:** KvK, industry certificates, partnerships

You don't need to use all forms. One concrete review in the right place can be more effective than ten on a separate page nobody visits.

## 7. Write a clear call-to-action

The call-to-action (CTA) is the action you ask of the visitor. It's surprising how many websites forget this or keep it vague.

**Bad CTAs:**
- "Contact us" (too vague — for what?)
- "Click here" (says nothing about what happens)
- "More information" (leads to another page instead of action)

**Good CTAs:**
- "Request a no-obligation quote"
- "Schedule a free introductory call"
- "Receive the price list by email"
- "View the demo"

A good CTA tells the visitor what they get, not what they have to do. "Receive your quote within 24 hours" is stronger than "Submit the form".

**Placement:** at minimum at the top of the page (above the fold) and at the bottom, after your convincing copy. On longer pages, also in between.

## 8. Read it aloud

The simplest test for website copy: read it aloud. If it sounds like a brochure or a legal document, rewrite it.

Good website copy sounds like a conversation. As if you're explaining what you do to a potential customer at the kitchen table, over a cup of coffee.

That means:
- Short sentences
- Plain words (not "implement" but "set up")
- Direct address ("you")
- No jargon unless your audience uses it

## Checklist per page

- [ ] The first sentence is about the visitor, not the company
- [ ] The problem is named before the solution appears
- [ ] Claims are backed by numbers, reviews, or examples
- [ ] There is one clear goal per page
- [ ] The CTA is specific and visible
- [ ] The copy is scannable (short paragraphs, subheadings, bullet lists)
- [ ] Reading aloud sounds natural

## How much copy does a page need?

There's no magic word count. But as a guideline:

| Page | Guideline |
|---|---|
| Homepage | 300–600 words |
| Services page | 500–1,000 words |
| About us | 300–500 words |
| Blog post (SEO) | 800–1,500 words |
| Product page (webshop) | 150–300 words |

More isn't always better. But too little copy gives Google insufficient context to understand and rank your page.

## Summary

- Copy determines whether visitors become customers — not design
- Write for the customer, not for yourself
- Be specific: numbers and proof convince, superlatives don't
- Make every page scannable and give every page one goal
- A good CTA tells the visitor what they get, not what they have to do
