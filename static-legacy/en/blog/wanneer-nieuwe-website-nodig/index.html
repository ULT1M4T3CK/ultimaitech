When is it time for a new website? 5 signs

25 May 2026

Wondering if your website still holds up? These 5 signs tell you whether it's time for a refresh — and when a touch-up is enough.

When is it time for a new website? 5 signs

Your website is running. Visitors come. Occasionally someone calls. But you have a gut feeling it could be better. That the site no longer fits where your business is now.

The question is: is that feeling justified? And if it is — do you need to replace the entire site, or is a refresh enough?

These are five concrete signs that it's time for a new website.

Sign 1: Your site is slow on mobile

Open your website on your phone. Does it load within two seconds? Is everything readable without zooming? Do buttons and forms work well with your thumb?

If the answer is no, you have a problem. More than 65% of all website visits in the Netherlands happen on a smartphone. Google indexes the mobile version of your site by default — not the desktop version.

When a refresh is enough: The site is responsive but slow. Then image optimization, caching, and removing unnecessary scripts can fix it.

When a new site is needed: The site was built for desktop and later "made responsive" with adjustments. That always involves compromises. A mobile-first rebuild is then the better investment.

Test it yourself: Open PageSpeed Insights, choose the mobile test, and enter your URL. A score below 50 is a clear sign.

Sign 2: You're not found on Google

You're invisible. When someone searches for what you do + your town name, you're not on page 1. Maybe not even page 2.

This usually has multiple causes at once:

  • Missing or poor meta titles and descriptions — Google doesn't know what your page is about
  • No sitemap or robots.txt — Google can't crawl your site efficiently
  • Thin content — too little text per page for Google to understand what you offer
  • No structured data — you miss opportunities for rich snippets (stars, prices, FAQ)
  • Technical problems — slow load time, broken links, duplicate content

When a refresh is enough: The technology is fine, but the content is thin. Then adding text, blog posts, and meta information is the solution.

When a new site is needed: The technology is the problem. Missing sitemaps, odd URL structures, inaccessible pages — if the foundation isn't right, refreshing is mopping with the tap running.

Test it yourself: Search Google for site:yourdomain.nl. The number of results tells you how many pages Google knows about. Is that less than you expect? Then there's a problem.

Sign 3: The site no longer fits your business

Your business has grown or changed. New services, new target audience, different positioning. But your website still tells the old story.

Examples I see regularly:

  • A landscaping company that started with garden maintenance and now also does paving, fencing, and ponds — but the site only mentions "garden maintenance"
  • A freelancer who has grown into a team, but the site still speaks in "I" form
  • A business that initially worked locally and now serves regionally or nationally, but the site only mentions their own town
  • A webshop that started with 20 products and now has 500, but the navigation is still set up for 20

When a refresh is enough: The technology and structure are good, but the text and images are outdated. Update the content and you're done.

When a new site is needed: The structure no longer fits. You need more pages, different navigation, or functionality the current site doesn't support.

Sign 4: You're not getting responses

Visitors come, but nobody gets in touch. No quote requests, no phone calls, no form submissions.

That can be because of:

  • Unclear call-to-actions — the visitor doesn't know what the next step is
  • Too much choice — three phone numbers, two email addresses, a form, and a WhatsApp button. The visitor chooses nothing
  • No trust — no reviews, no KvK number, no face behind the business
  • The form doesn't work — sounds unlikely, but it happens more often than you think. Test it regularly
  • The wrong visitors — you're attracting traffic that doesn't match your target audience

When a refresh is enough: The site attracts the right visitors, but conversion is low. Clearer CTAs, trust signals, and a better contact page can make the difference.

When a new site is needed: The site attracts the wrong audience, or the user experience is so poor that visitors drop off before seeing your offer.

Test it yourself: Ask five people (not your partner or your mother) to find how to contact you on your site. See how long it takes and where they get stuck.

Sign 5: You're embarrassed to share the link

The most honest test. Do you proudly share your website link when someone asks? Or do you add "it's a bit outdated" or "we're working on a new one"?

If you can't share your website with confidence, that's a sign you can't argue away. Your website is your digital business card. If you don't stand behind it, your customers won't either.

The lifespan of a website

A website isn't a building that lasts 30 years. Average lifespan is:

Type of website Average lifespan
Template/WordPress site 2–4 years
Custom business site 4–6 years
Webshop 3–5 years
Web application 5–8 years

After this period, the technology is outdated, the design dated, and the content no longer current. A refresh isn't a luxury then, but an investment in the continuity of your online presence.

Refresh versus rebuild

Aspect Refresh Rebuild
Cost €500 – €3,000 €2,500 – €8,000
Timeline 1–3 weeks 4–10 weeks
Result Improved version Completely new
When Content outdated, design fine Technology outdated, structure doesn't fit
Risk May still need rebuild later Higher upfront investment

My advice: if two or more of the five signs apply to you, rebuilding is almost always the better choice. You invest more, but you start with a clean slate and a site that will serve you for the next 4–6 years.

What can you do today?

  1. Open your site on your phone and honestly assess the experience
  2. Search Google for your business name + town name. Are you in position 1?
  3. Compare your site with your direct competitor's. Who comes across as more professional?
  4. Check your contact form — send a test message and see if you receive it
  5. Ask an outsider to assess your site in 30 seconds

If the results don't satisfy you, it's time to take action.

Summary

  • Slow on mobile, invisible on Google, or no responses = time for action
  • If your business has changed but your site hasn't, you're missing customers
  • A refresh is enough if only the content is outdated
  • Rebuilding is better if the technology or structure is the problem
  • A website lasts on average 3–6 years — after that, renewal is needed
